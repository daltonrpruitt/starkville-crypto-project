## Power Usage
The project requests a power load of 30 MW[^1]. 
Atwell and Gent (the engineering consulting firm that does work with the city) was contracted to do a power study to make sure this power draw would not put strain on existing infrastructure, and they determined there was "ample capacity in the substation serving the load to serve it and retain ample for 10+ years of growth."[^2] 
The substation that would serve this project's load is rated for 50MVA (mega volt-amps; basically the same as MW, I think?), and the current load is only using 3-5MW used by Southwire. (See work session for info). 

Some other quotes from the Fact Sheet:
- "The customer is proposing to be a demand response customer which means they will curtail or shut down during certain peak periods as requested by SU (Starkville Utilities) or TVA."
- "The customer will be responsible for paying for all improvements to serve the site which includes substation and distribution upgrades totaling $792,641"
- "SU/ Rate payers will not pay for any improvements, obligations or bear any financial risk associated with the project."

### A quick comparison of cooling methods

A very important component of any computer system is the cooling system. 
There are several different cooling systems that can be used, but the two of interest to us to demonstrate are:
- air cooling, where fans blow "cool" air over the computers, the air absorbs some of the heat from the computers,  and then the heated air is exhausted back into the atmosphere
- evaporative cooling, where hot air is circulated over some water-soaked structure, where the water cools the air by absorbing heat as it evaporates (this is basically how we sweat, too). 

The site in Columbus, MS uses air cooling, and you have to use a lot of fan power for that. The noise from that site is loud. 
While it is close to the airport, plane noise is at least not constant. 
I have not heard anything from nearby residents about it.

The site proposed for Starkville is supposed to use some form of evaporative cooling, which requires much less fan power to achieve the same level of cooling. 
Therefore, it should be quieter.
From the Fact Sheet:
> Proposed project has a cooling system/fans that recirculate water through a chiller system. Water usage will be “makeup” water to resupply the cooling system due to evaporation, especially during summer months.

#### Infrasound

One of the larger concerns of mine is sound that is outside the [range of normal human hearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_range), so below ~20 Hz and above ~20,000 Hz (Hz = hertz, or "cycles per second").[^5] 

Frequencies below human hearing are called "infrasonic sounds" or "infrasound", and have been associated with potential healing ability when controlled appropriately and potential harm when through chronic exposure depending on intensity and frequency ([article](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3417/16/3/1553)). 
Further, some residents living near [data centers](https://www.bbc.com/news/articles/c93dnnxewdvo) have reported negative health effects. 
This is such an interesting phenomenon, some [internet people](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_bP80DEAbuo) are investigating it, so you know it's a big deal! 😉

### To animals

As shown in [this diagram](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/9/95/Animal_hearing_frequency_range_logarithmic.svg/1920px-Animal_hearing_frequency_range_logarithmic.svg.png) of the [Hearing range Wikipedia article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearing_range), different animals have different ranges of hearing, with most animals being able to hear at much higher frequencies than humans can. 
As such, there are certain sounds that animals can hear that we can't; this is the idea behind dog whistles. 

So, if is possible that the proposed site could produce noise at higher frequencies that animals could hear, it is important that such sounds be mitigated to avoid distress to local wildlife and residents of the Oktibbeha County Humane Society, as it will be relatively close by.

## This isn't just about our own health and safety - environmental concerns

As mentioned above, the production of power in the U.S. is still heavily dependent on non-renewable/non-clean energy sources, such as coal, natural gas, fuel oil, and others. 
While nuclear is technically non-renewable, it can be counted as "clean" in the sense that not as many emissions are generated during it's operation (and even when you amortize the construction emissions it is still much better than fossil fuel emissions[^4]),. 
Based on [TVA's Draft Integrated Resource Plan for 2025](https://www.tva.com/environment/draft-2025-integrated-resource-plan), they included a Draft [Environmental Impact Statement](https://tva-azr-eastus-cdn-ep-tvawcm-prd.azureedge.net/cdn-tvawcma/docs/default-source/environment/environmental-stewardship/integrated-resource-plan/2025/draft-2025-environmental-impact-statement.pdf?sfvrsn=d923248c_1) that gives the following table for 2020-2023 generation by type of production:

![[Pasted image 20260723115552.png]]

Based on this table, I worked out that the "non-clean" to "clean" power production is as follows:

| Generation Category | FY 2023 | FY 2022 | FY 2021 | FY 2020 |
| ------------------- | ------- | ------- | ------- | ------- |
| Non-renewable       | 44.93%  | 47.36%  | 43.74%  | 42.23%  |
| Renewable/”Clean”   | 55.07%  | 52.64%  | 56.26%  | 57.77%  |

Based on this, we can see that non-renewables have made up approximately 45% of the power generation of TVA. 
At that rate, to generate 1 ton (2000 lbs) of CO2e would take between approximately 4.7 and 6.4 minutes, and that does not account for transfer losses and other minor inefficiencies.
